Holistic health

Holistic health

Specific populations

Exact syllabus scope
  • Characteristics and needs of specific populations
  • Access and equity issues of specific populations

Specific populations are groups sharing characteristics, needs or risk factors that shape distinctive health needs. Relevant examples in the supplied notes include children and young people, CALD and migrant groups, men and women, refugees, older people, LGBTQIA+ people, rural and remote populations, First Nations peoples and people in prison. Their needs are identified using health data and by examining barriers to access and equity.

Access versus equity

Access is the ability to obtain and use health resources when needed. Equity is fair distribution of resources and opportunities according to need, so groups facing greater barriers may require greater support.

Examples from your notes

Rural/remote populations may face limited facilities, long travel distances, workforce shortages and higher costs. Older people may face mobility barriers, caregiver shortages and a digital divide. First Nations peoples may face limited culturally safe services, historical trauma and higher burden of chronic disease. CALD populations may face language barriers, unfamiliarity with the system and culturally mismatched care. LGBTQIA+ people may face discrimination or lack of affirming care. People in prison may have high health need but constrained choice and continuity of care.

Factors creating health inequities

Exact syllabus scope
  • Discrimination
  • Gender
  • Access to health care
  • Unemployment
  • Social isolation
  • Dislocation of land
  • Occupation
  • Access to and level of education
  • Geographic location
  • Racism
  • Government economic and social policies
  • Socioeconomic status
  • Health literacy

Health inequities can be created by interacting social, economic, geographic and health-system factors. The syllabus requires you to know how discrimination, gender, healthcare access, unemployment, social isolation, dislocation of land, occupation, education, geographic location, racism, government policy, socioeconomic status and health literacy can produce unequal opportunities for health.

How the factors create inequity

Discrimination/racism can reduce trust, create stress and restrict access to employment or services. Gender can shape exposure to violence, income, caregiving, health expectations and service access. Healthcare access affects prevention, diagnosis and treatment. Unemployment reduces income and may increase stress and social exclusion. Social isolation reduces emotional/practical support. Dislocation from land can damage cultural, spiritual and social wellbeing and disrupt community support.

Resources and opportunity

Occupation affects income, conditions, status and exposure to hazards. Education shapes employment opportunity and health literacy. Geographic location influences service availability, transport and environmental exposure. Government policy shapes taxation, welfare, housing, education and service distribution. SES reflects access to material and social resources. Health literacy shapes navigation, understanding and use of health information.

Measuring health inequities

Exact syllabus scope
  • Quantitative and qualitative measures for detecting health inequities and/or injustices
  • Epidemiological data
  • Social determinants of health

Health inequities and injustices can be detected using quantitative and qualitative measures. Quantitative evidence shows the size and pattern of a health gap, while qualitative evidence explains experiences, perceptions and barriers. Epidemiological data and evidence about social determinants of health are central sources.

Quantitative

Numerical, measurable information. In health this includes life expectancy, incidence, prevalence, mortality, morbidity, hospitalisation, screening uptake or service-use rates. Compare the same indicator across populations, time or place.

Qualitative

Non-numerical information about experiences, perceptions, beliefs and barriers, gathered through interviews, focus groups, open-ended surveys, case studies or community consultation.

Principles, frameworks, models & theories

Socio-ecological model

Exact syllabus scope
  • Individual
  • Interpersonal
  • Organisational
  • Community
  • Society
  • Role in understanding and addressing public health problems

The socio-ecological model explains health as the product of interacting influences at five levels: individual, interpersonal, organisational, community and society. It is used to understand why public-health problems have multiple causes and why action at several levels can be needed.

Individual

Personal knowledge, attitudes, beliefs, skills, biology and behaviour.

Interpersonal

Family, friends, peers and close social networks that influence norms, support and behaviour.

Organisational

Structures, rules and practices within settings such as schools, workplaces, health services and sporting organisations.

Community

Relationships among organisations, neighbourhoods and local networks; community norms, resources and built environment.

Society

Broader laws, policies, economic conditions, media, cultural norms and systems.

Why it matters

A public-health intervention is usually stronger when it combines levels. Education may improve knowledge, but policy, service availability and social norms determine whether people can act on it.

Social justice principles

Exact syllabus scope
  • Access and equity
  • Diversity
  • Supportive environments

The syllabus identifies three social justice principles in health: access and equity, diversity, and supportive environments. Together they guide fair health action by reducing barriers, recognising different needs and creating conditions that support health.

Access & equity

Remove financial, geographic, cultural, physical or informational barriers and allocate additional support where need is greater.

Diversity

Recognise differences in culture, language, age, ability, gender, identity and circumstances; design services so difference does not become disadvantage.

Supportive environments

Create physical, social and policy conditions that make health-promoting choices and service use easier and safer.

Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs

Exact syllabus scope
  • Purpose and characteristics of the five levels of need
  • Physiological
  • Safety
  • Love and belonging
  • Esteem
  • Self-actualisation

Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs organises human needs into five levels: physiological, safety, love and belonging, esteem and self-actualisation. The model is used to explain how unmet lower-level needs can affect priorities, behaviour and wellbeing.

1 · Physiological

Basic survival: food, water, sleep, warmth and other bodily requirements.

2 · Safety

Security, stability, shelter, protection from harm and access to conditions that preserve health.

3 · Love & belonging

Relationships, friendship, connection, acceptance and belonging.

4 · Esteem

Self-respect, competence, confidence, recognition and achievement.

5 · Self-actualisation

Personal growth, creativity, meaning and reaching one’s potential.

PABCAR model

Exact syllabus scope
  • Identification of the problem
  • Amenability to change
  • Benefits and costs of implementing interventions
  • Acceptability of proposed measures
  • Recommended actions and monitoring

PABCAR is a public-health decision-making model. It moves from identifying a problem, to judging amenability to change, weighing benefits and costs, assessing acceptability, then recommending actions and monitoring outcomes.

P — Identification of the problem

Define the health issue, who is affected, magnitude, trend and consequences using epidemiological and other evidence.

A — Amenability to change

Ask whether the problem or its determinants can realistically be modified, and whether effective interventions exist.

B/C — Benefits and costs

Compare expected health, social and economic benefits with financial, workforce, time and opportunity costs.

A — Acceptability

Assess whether the target population, community, professionals and decision-makers are likely to support and use the measure.

R — Recommended actions & monitoring

Choose actions, implement them, specify indicators and timeframes, and monitor whether outcomes improve.

Actions & strategies

Needs assessment

Exact syllabus scope
  • Purpose of a needs assessment
  • Comparative need
  • Felt need
  • Expressed need
  • Normative need
  • Identifying health issues
  • Analysis of the problem
  • Prioritising issues
  • Setting goals
  • Determining strategies
  • Developing action plans
  • Evaluating outcomes

A needs assessment identifies and prioritises population health needs so appropriate goals, strategies and actions can be planned. The syllabus requires four types of need and the seven steps of the needs-assessment process.

Four types of need

Felt = what people perceive or say they need. Expressed = a felt need converted into action or demand, such as service use or a waiting list. Normative = need identified by an expert or professional standard. Comparative = need inferred by comparing similar populations and finding unequal provision or outcomes.

Seven-step process

1) Identify health issues using data and consultation. 2) Analyse causes, risk/protective factors and existing resources. 3) Prioritise issues using severity, size, feasibility and community importance. 4) Set clear goals. 5) Determine evidence-based strategies. 6) Develop an action plan with responsibilities, resources and timelines. 7) Evaluate outcomes and use results to improve the response.

What evaluation should contain

Link evaluation to the original goal. Specify an indicator, data source and timeframe; distinguish process measures (was the program delivered?) from outcome measures (did health/access/behaviour change?).

Actions & strategies

Ottawa Charter

Exact syllabus scope
  • Enabling, mediating and advocating strategies in the Ottawa Charter to reduce inequities of specific groups
  • Actions to improve access to healthcare
  • Actions to improve health literacy
  • Ottawa Charter action areas

The Ottawa Charter uses enabling, mediating and advocating strategies to reduce inequities. The syllabus also requires the five Ottawa Charter action areas and actions that improve access to healthcare and health literacy.

Enable

Reduce differences in health status and give people resources/opportunities to achieve their health potential. Examples: free screening, transport support, accessible information, skills programs.

Mediate

Coordinate and reconcile different interests across governments, health services, communities, industry and other sectors. Example: local government, a school and health service agree on a shared youth health initiative.

Advocate

Act for political, social, economic or environmental conditions that support health, especially where a group lacks power or resources.

Five action areas

Build healthy public policy; Create supportive environments; Strengthen community action; Develop personal skills; Reorient health services.

Improving access

Examples from the supplied notes include free/subsidised appointments, clinics in accessible locations, mobile services, telehealth, extended hours, home visits, transport support and bilingual/interpreter staff.

Improving health literacy

Use plain language and jargon-free resources, visual information, culturally relevant examples, translated materials, trusted community delivery and opportunities to ask questions or check understanding.

Rio Declaration

Exact syllabus scope
  • Actions to achieve social and health equity in the Rio Declaration on Social Determinants of Health

The Rio Political Declaration on Social Determinants of Health sets out actions for achieving greater social and health equity by acting on the conditions that shape health across populations.

1 · Adopt better governance for health and development

Use transparent, accountable and health-conscious decision-making across government; include health equity in policy beyond the health department.

2 · Promote participation in policy-making and implementation

Enable communities, civil society and affected groups to influence decisions that shape their health.

3 · Further reorient the health sector toward reducing health inequities

Strengthen prevention, primary care, equitable access and health workforce awareness of social determinants.

4 · Strengthen global governance and collaboration

Coordinate countries and international organisations on determinants and health problems that cross borders.

5 · Monitor progress and increase accountability

Collect and disaggregate data, set indicators, report progress and hold decision-makers accountable for inequities.

Healthcare system reforms

Exact syllabus scope
  • Private health insurance rebate
  • Public screening and/or vaccination programs
  • Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS)

The syllabus requires three Australian healthcare-system reforms: the private health insurance rebate, public screening and/or vaccination programs, and the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS). Know the purpose of each and how it affects access to care or prevention.

Private Health Insurance Rebate

A government contribution toward eligible private health insurance premiums. Its purpose is to encourage private cover and share demand between private and public services. explain the intended system-level mechanism rather than claiming it automatically eliminates public waiting times.

Public screening and vaccination programs

Organised, publicly supported programs can reduce cost and awareness barriers, increase early detection or immunity and improve population coverage. Examples include national cancer screening programs and immunisation programs.

Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS)

The Australian Government subsidises listed prescription medicines so patients pay a subsidised amount rather than the full market cost; concession arrangements and the Safety Net further protect people with high medicine costs.

Attitudinal & environmental influences

Culture, BAV & healthcare

Exact syllabus scope
  • Influence of culture on personal beliefs, attitudes and values towards healthcare
  • Influence of geographical location on health behaviour of cultural groups
  • Influence of social networks on health behaviour of cultural groups

Culture can shape personal beliefs, attitudes and values towards healthcare. Geographic location and social networks can also influence the health behaviour of cultural groups by affecting access, support, information, expectations and trust.

Beliefs

Ideas accepted as true, such as beliefs about causes of illness, traditional healing or prevention. Beliefs can influence which treatments a person trusts or seeks first.

Attitudes

Positive, negative or neutral evaluations of healthcare, shaped by experience, family, media and culture. Prior discrimination can contribute to distrust.

Values

Enduring principles that guide decisions, such as autonomy, family responsibility, modesty, preservation of life or cultural continuity.

Geographical location

Even when a cultural group wants care, remoteness can limit culturally appropriate services, interpreters, transport and provider choice.

Social networks

Family, friends, elders and community can transmit norms, information and support. They can encourage help-seeking or, where stigma is present, discourage it.

Attitudinal & environmental influences

Norm conflict

Exact syllabus scope
  • Conflict between norms of specific groups and majority norms

Conflict can occur when the norms of a specific group differ from majority norms. This can affect acceptance of health advice, trust, communication, stigma and willingness to use health services.

How conflict develops

A health service may assume majority norms around individual decision-making, eye contact, disclosure, gender of practitioner or treatment. A patient may operate within different cultural expectations. If neither side recognises the difference, behaviour can be misread as non-compliance, disrespect or lack of interest.

Health consequences

Misunderstanding → reduced trust → less disclosure or attendance → delayed diagnosis/treatment → poorer health outcomes. Culturally safe practice, interpreters and shared decision-making can reduce the conflict.

Self-management skills

Exact syllabus scope
  • Assertiveness
  • Stress management
  • Resilience

The three self-management skills in the syllabus are assertiveness, stress management and resilience. Each can support positive health behaviour by helping a person communicate needs, cope with demands and adapt to setbacks.

Assertiveness

Clear, confident and respectful communication of needs, rights and boundaries. It can protect health by resisting pressure while preserving relationships.

Stress management

Strategies that reduce the physical, emotional and behavioural effects of stress, such as planning, relaxation, sleep routines, social support and problem solving.

Resilience

Capacity to adapt, recover and continue functioning after adversity. Protective factors can include social support, coping skills, self-efficacy and access to resources.

Culture & health decisions

Exact syllabus scope
  • Impact of culture on organ and tissue donation
  • Impact of culture on blood transfusions
  • Impact of culture on childbirth

Culture can influence decisions about organ and tissue donation, blood transfusions and childbirth through beliefs, values, religious or spiritual practices, family roles and expectations about healthcare.

Organ & tissue donation

Beliefs about bodily wholeness after death, death rituals, spiritual teachings, family decision-making and trust in institutions may influence consent.

Blood transfusion

Religious beliefs, ideas about bodily purity, risk perception and understanding of alternatives may influence acceptance. Clinicians need clear communication and informed consent.

Childbirth

Culture may influence preferred birth setting, family/elder involvement, gender of practitioner, pain management, rituals, attitudes toward intervention and communication style.

Relationship building in health settings

Exact syllabus scope
  • Language and cultural influences on relationship building in health settings

Language and culture influence relationship building in health settings. Clear communication, mutual understanding and respect can strengthen trust and rapport, while language barriers or culturally mismatched communication can weaken them.

Language

Limited shared language can reduce accuracy of symptom description, understanding of risk, informed consent and treatment adherence. Professional interpreters can reduce these risks.

Cultural communication

Eye contact, directness, silence, touch, personal space, gender expectations and family involvement can carry different meanings. Practitioners should ask, listen and adapt rather than assume.

Trust and rapport

Trust improves disclosure and follow-up. Prior discrimination or culturally unsafe care can undermine trust even when the service is technically available.

Determinants of health inequities

Exact syllabus scope
  • Social: social gradient, stress, early life, social exclusion, work, unemployment, social support, addiction, food, transport, culture
  • Environmental: features of natural and built environment, geographical location
  • Socioeconomic: education, employment, income, family, housing/neighbourhood, access to services, migration/refugee status, food security
  • Biomedical: birth weight, body weight

The syllabus groups determinants linked to health inequities into social, environmental, socioeconomic and biomedical determinants. Know each listed determinant and how it can influence exposure, resources, behaviour, service access and health outcomes.

Social determinants

Social gradient: health generally improves at each step up the social hierarchy. Stress: chronic stress can affect mental health, blood pressure and health behaviour. Early life: nutrition, stimulation, safety and care can shape development and later health. Social exclusion: reduces participation, access and belonging. Work/unemployment: job security, control, hazards, income, purpose and stress matter. Social support: networks can buffer stress and aid recovery. Addiction: can both result from and intensify disadvantage. Food/transport/culture: shape access, behaviour, connection and service engagement.

Environmental determinants

The natural environment includes climate, air, water and exposure to hazards. The built environment includes housing, roads, transport, green space, walkability and service locations. Geographical location changes exposure and access to resources.

Socioeconomic determinants

Education, employment and income shape material resources and opportunity. Family and housing affect safety and support. Access to services determines whether needs can be met. Migration/refugee status can introduce language barriers, trauma, discrimination and disrupted networks. Food security determines reliable access to sufficient safe and nutritious food.

Biomedical determinants

Low birth weight is associated with greater risk of infant illness/death and later developmental or chronic health problems. Body weight, especially excess body weight in context, is associated with increased risk of conditions such as type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease. Biomedical factors interact with social and environmental determinants rather than replacing them.

Global & local barriers

Exact syllabus scope
  • Poverty
  • Disease outbreaks
  • Famine
  • Drought
  • Availability of clean drinking water

Poverty, disease outbreaks, famine, drought and limited access to clean drinking water can block action on the social determinants of health by reducing resources, damaging infrastructure and worsening existing disadvantage.

Poverty

Insufficient resources restrict food, housing, education, transport and healthcare. Poor health can then reduce capacity to work or study, reinforcing poverty.

Disease outbreaks

Outbreaks increase morbidity/mortality, strain health services, disrupt work/school and divert scarce resources from routine care. Fear, stigma and movement restrictions can worsen social exclusion.

Famine

Extreme food shortage produces acute malnutrition and increases susceptibility to disease; it can also cause migration, conflict over resources and long-term developmental effects in children.

Drought

Reduces water availability and agricultural production, threatening food security and livelihoods. The health impact is greater where infrastructure, income and social protection are weak.

Clean drinking water

Unsafe or unavailable water increases infectious disease risk, time spent collecting water and barriers to school/work, especially where sanitation is also inadequate.

Local vs global comparison

The same barrier can have different health effects depending on health-system capacity, infrastructure, income, governance, social protection and existing inequity.

Australia’s aid program

Exact syllabus scope
  • Humanitarian policy, with a focus on humanitarian action principles
  • Gender equality, incorporating the five strategic priorities

Australia’s aid program is examined through humanitarian policy and gender equality. Know why Australia provides humanitarian assistance, the humanitarian action principles, and the five gender-equality strategic priorities specified for the course.

Why Australia provides aid

Humanitarian aid aims to save lives, alleviate suffering, protect dignity and support people affected by conflict, disaster and other crises. Development assistance can strengthen regional health, stability and resilience, which also supports Australia’s strategic interest in a safer and more prosperous region.

Humanitarian action principles

Humanity: prevent and alleviate suffering and protect life/dignity. Neutrality: do not take sides in hostilities or political/religious controversies. Impartiality: provide assistance based on need without adverse discrimination. Independence: keep humanitarian objectives autonomous from political, economic, military or other objectives.

Five gender equality strategic priorities

1) End sexual and gender-based violence and advance/protect women’s sexual and reproductive health and rights. 2) Pursue gender-responsive peace and security efforts. 3) Deliver gender-equitable climate action and humanitarian assistance. 4) Promote women’s economic equality and inclusive trade. 5) Support locally led approaches to women’s leadership.

Sustainable Development Goals

Exact syllabus scope
  • Goal 2: End hunger, achieve food security and improved nutrition and promote sustainable agriculture
  • Goal 3: Ensure healthy lives and promote well-being for all at all ages
  • Goal 4: Ensure inclusive and equitable quality education and promote lifelong learning opportunities for all
  • Goal 5: Achieve gender equality and empower all women and girls
  • Goal 6: Ensure availability and sustainable management of water and sanitation for all
  • Purpose of, and progress towards, each goal

The syllabus requires the purpose of, and progress towards, Sustainable Development Goals 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6. These cover hunger and food security, health and wellbeing, education, gender equality, and water and sanitation.

Goal 2 · Zero Hunger

Purpose: end hunger, achieve food security and improved nutrition, and promote sustainable agriculture. Health link: adequate nutrition reduces malnutrition and susceptibility to illness; sustainable food systems improve resilience. Progress: global hunger has declined gradually in recent years, but progress remains too slow and is threatened by conflict, extreme weather and reduced humanitarian funding.

Goal 3 · Good Health & Well-being

Purpose: ensure healthy lives and promote wellbeing for all ages. It spans maternal/child health, communicable and non-communicable diseases, mental health, road safety, universal health coverage and essential medicines. Progress has occurred in many health outcomes, but global gains remain uneven and insufficient for 2030.

Goal 4 · Quality Education

Purpose: inclusive and equitable quality education and lifelong learning. Education improves health literacy, employment, income and empowerment. Global access has improved over decades, but learning outcomes, completion and inequality remain major barriers.

Goal 5 · Gender Equality

Purpose: achieve gender equality and empower women and girls. Health link: power, education, income, freedom from violence and reproductive autonomy shape health. Progress: women hold 27.4% of parliamentary seats globally and progress toward parity remains slow; violence and discriminatory barriers persist.

Goal 6 · Clean Water & Sanitation

Purpose: universal, sustainable access to safe water, sanitation and hygiene. Health link: reduces waterborne disease and time burdens. Progress: by 2024, 74% of the global population used safely managed drinking water and 58% safely managed sanitation, showing gains but a substantial gap remains.

Health promotion advocacy

Exact syllabus scope

Health promotion advocacy seeks to influence decisions, public opinion, policy or resource allocation to improve health. The syllabus requires when advocacy is best used and nine named advocacy strategies.

Definition & when to use it

Health promotion advocacy is action that supports an issue, policy or population to influence decision-makers, policy, resources or public opinion in favour of health. Use it when education alone cannot remove the barrier.

Lobbying

Directly communicate with a decision-maker to request a specific change. Strong lobbying has a clear ask, evidence, target and feasible action.

Raising awareness

Increase knowledge and visibility of an issue so people recognise its importance.

Creating debate

Place the issue into public or political discussion, often by challenging assumptions or presenting competing evidence.

Developing partnerships

Build relationships between organisations or sectors that contribute expertise, legitimacy, reach or resources.

Building capacity

Strengthen a group’s skills, knowledge, resources and structures so it can act or advocate more effectively over time.

Mobilising groups

Organise people to take collective action, demonstrating support and increasing pressure or visibility.

Framing issues

Present the same issue through a strategically chosen lens—equity, cost, rights, safety, productivity—to make it meaningful to the target audience without distorting evidence.

Using champions

Use credible or influential individuals to attract attention, build trust and amplify a message.

Influencing policy

Seek formal change to rules, funding, institutional practice or government policy so benefits can reach a population at scale.

Health indicators

Exact syllabus scope
  • Life expectancy
  • Mortality
  • Morbidity

Australia and developing countries can be compared using life expectancy, mortality and morbidity. These indicators show differences in population health status and disease burden.

Life expectancy

Average number of years a person is expected to live under current mortality patterns. Australia generally has high life expectancy because of strong sanitation, nutrition, education, health services and living standards, though inequities exist within Australia.

Mortality

Frequency of death in a population, including general or cause-specific rates and measures such as infant/maternal mortality. Many lower-resource settings experience higher preventable mortality where safe childbirth, vaccination, sanitation or timely treatment are less accessible.

Morbidity

Illness, disease or disability in a population. Both Australia and developing countries experience chronic and infectious disease; the relative pattern differs by age structure, epidemiological transition, exposures and service access.

Attitudinal & environmental influences

Government policy & regulation

Exact syllabus scope
  • Influence of government policies and regulations on beliefs, attitudes and values
  • Government policies and regulations that restrict or promote healthy behaviour

Government policies and regulations can shape beliefs, attitudes and values and can either restrict unhealthy behaviour or promote healthy behaviour.

Policy vs regulation

A policy is a guiding course of action or strategy. A regulation is a legally enforceable rule made under government authority. Some health initiatives combine both policy and law.

Restrict unhealthy behaviour

Examples include tobacco controls, age restrictions, drink-driving laws, speed limits or smoke-free rules. Enforcement changes incentives and exposure; over time repeated compliance and public messaging can shift social norms and attitudes.

Promote healthy behaviour

Subsidies, screening/vaccination programs, health education, built-environment policy and service funding can make healthy action easier, cheaper or more normal.

Influence on BAV

Policies can change beliefs by communicating evidence, attitudes by changing social approval/disapproval, and values by reframing issues around shared responsibility, safety or fairness.

Attitudinal & environmental influences

PPP norms

Exact syllabus scope
  • Relationship between health behaviours and proscriptive, prescriptive and popular norms

Proscriptive, prescriptive and popular norms influence health behaviour by signalling what people should not do, should do, or commonly do.

Proscriptive

Rules or expectations against a behaviour: ‘Do not vape at school.’ They can discourage harmful behaviour through disapproval or sanctions.

Prescriptive

Rules or expectations in favour of a behaviour: ‘Wear sunscreen’ or ‘attend screening’. They create expectations about positive action.

Popular

Perceptions or observations of what most people actually do. Popular norms can support or undermine official health messages—for example, a young person may think vaping is common even if adults strongly disapprove.

Why norms matter

People use others’ behaviour and approval as social information. Perceived norms can alter risk perception, belonging and willingness to comply with health advice.

Displacement from traditional homelands, war, violence, conflict and natural disasters can affect personal, social and cultural identity by disrupting safety, relationships, roles, culture, place and community connection.

Displacement from traditional homelands

Can sever connection to place/Country, family networks, language, cultural practice and livelihood. Resettlement may add language barriers, discrimination and altered roles.

War & conflict

Can cause trauma, bereavement, disrupted education, family separation, migration, poverty and breakdown of institutions. It can strengthen some group identities while fragmenting others.

Violence

Exposure can alter sense of safety, trust and self-concept and increase risk of injury and trauma-related mental-health problems.

Natural disasters

Loss of home, community infrastructure and livelihoods can disrupt social roles and place-based identity. Collective recovery can also strengthen community solidarity.

Communication & collaboration skills

Exact syllabus scope

Health settings use mediation, negotiation, compromise, conflict management, arbitration, leadership and facilitation to support effective communication and collaboration.

Mediation

A neutral third party facilitates communication so the parties can reach their own voluntary agreement.

Negotiation

The parties communicate directly to reach a mutually acceptable outcome.

Compromise

Each party concedes part of its preferred outcome in order to reach agreement.

Managing conflict

Recognise the issue, maintain respectful communication, clarify interests, de-escalate emotion and work toward a constructive resolution.

Arbitration

A neutral third party considers the dispute and makes a decision, often binding under the agreed process.

Leadership

Guide, motivate and coordinate people toward a shared health goal; may involve vision, role modelling, delegation and accountability.

Facilitation

Guide a group process so participants can contribute, stay focused and reach an outcome without the facilitator necessarily deciding the content.

Skills & processes

Health inquiry

Exact syllabus scope
  • Identification and analysis of a health issue
  • Development of focus questions
  • Use of a range of reliable information sources
  • Criteria for selecting information sources
  • Summary of information
  • Identification and analysis of trends and patterns in data
  • Development of argument
  • Development of evidence-based conclusions
  • Presentation of findings in appropriate format to suit audience

Health inquiry involves planning a focused investigation, selecting reliable information, interpreting evidence, developing an argument and evidence-based conclusions, and presenting findings in a form suited to the intended audience.

1 · Plan

Identify and analyse a health issue, define the population and scope, then develop focused research questions. Good focus questions are specific, researchable and analytically useful rather than yes/no.

2 · Select sources

Use a range of credible sources. Assess authority, currency, accuracy/evidence, purpose/bias, relevance and, where relevant, methodology and sample.

3 · Interpret

Summarise rather than copy. Identify trends (direction over time), patterns (relationships, differences, recurring features), anomalies and limitations. Compare sources and explain plausible mechanisms.

4 · Build an argument

Make a claim that directly answers the focus question, support it with selected evidence, explain how the evidence supports the claim, consider limitations or alternative explanations, then draw an evidence-based conclusion.

5 · Present for audience

Choose format, language, visuals and level of detail for the audience. A policy brief, infographic, oral presentation and scientific report require different density, tone and evidence presentation.
